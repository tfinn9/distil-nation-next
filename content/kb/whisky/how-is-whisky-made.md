Whisky production follows a consistent core process, whether it's Scotch, Irish, American or New Zealand whisky.

## 1. Malting (for malted grain whiskies)

Barley is soaked in water to germinate, which activates enzymes that convert starch into fermentable sugar. It's then dried (traditionally over a kiln, sometimes with peat smoke — see "What is peated whisky?") to stop germination at the right point.

## 2. Mashing

The malted (or unmalted, for grain whisky) cereal is milled and mixed with hot water to extract fermentable sugars, producing a porridge-like liquid called the mash. The liquid drawn off is called wort.

## 3. Fermentation

Yeast is added to the wort and left to ferment for roughly two to four days, converting sugar into alcohol and producing a beer-like liquid (around 7–9% ABV) called the "wash."

## 4. Distillation

The wash is distilled — usually twice in pot stills (the traditional Scotch malt whisky approach) or through continuous column stills (common for grain whisky and bourbon) — to concentrate the alcohol and separate out unwanted flavour compounds. See our article on heads, hearts and tails for how distillers decide which part of the run to keep.

## 5. Maturation

The distilled spirit ("new make") is filled into oak barrels and left to age. This is where whisky gets almost all of its colour and a large share of its final flavour — see our article on how barrel ageing works. Most whisky-producing countries set a legal minimum ageing period (commonly three years), though New Zealand does not currently have a single universally enforced minimum the way Scotland does under Scotch Whisky Regulations.

## 6. Blending, cutting to strength and bottling

Before bottling, the matured spirit may be blended across casks (for consistency) or bottled as a single cask. Water is typically added to bring the spirit down from barrel strength to the final bottling ABV, unless it's bottled at "cask strength."
