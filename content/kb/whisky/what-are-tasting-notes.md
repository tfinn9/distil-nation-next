Tasting notes are simply a way of writing down what a spirit smells and tastes like, broken into a few standard stages so descriptions are easier to compare across different bottles.

## The usual structure

- **Nose (aroma)** — what you smell before tasting. This is where a huge amount of a spirit's character actually lives, since smell drives most of what we perceive as flavour.
- **Palate (taste)** — what you experience in the mouth: sweetness, spice, fruit, texture, intensity.
- **Finish** — what lingers after you swallow (or spit) — how long it lasts, and whether new flavours emerge as it fades.

Some tasting notes also comment on **colour** (though this can be affected by additives like caramel colouring in some spirits, so it's a weaker indicator of quality or age than people sometimes assume) and **texture/mouthfeel** (oily, thin, creamy, hot).

## Common descriptors

Tasting notes tend to borrow language from other foods and materials because spirits rarely have single, easily nameable flavours: vanilla, honey, toffee, dried fruit, orchard fruit, citrus, baking spice, smoke, leather, nuts, grass, pepper.

## Why they're useful (and where they fall short)

Tasting notes help communicate a spirit's general character and help you build a mental map of styles you like. But they're inherently subjective — different tasters, different glassware, different room temperatures and even what you ate beforehand can all shift what you perceive. Treat any tasting note (including your own) as a rough guide, not an objective measurement.
