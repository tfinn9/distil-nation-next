Unlike home-brewing beer or making your own wine — both generally permitted in New Zealand for personal, non-commercial use — **distilling spirits at home is treated very differently**. Producing spirits requires a licence under the Customs and Excise Act 2018, and this generally applies regardless of whether the spirit is intended for sale or purely personal consumption.

## Why spirits are treated more strictly than beer or wine

Excise duty on alcohol is charged per litre of pure alcohol, and spirits carry a substantially higher effective rate than an equivalent volume of beer or wine. That gap gives unlicensed spirit production a much bigger potential impact on excise revenue than home-brewing does, which is a major reason the two are regulated so differently.

## Safety is also a factor

Distillation done without proper knowledge and equipment carries real safety risks — most notably the risk of methanol contamination if the "cut" (see our article on heads, hearts and tails) isn't managed correctly, which can cause serious harm. This is part of the broader public-interest case for licensing, alongside the tax rationale.

## What this means practically

If you want to distil spirits — even a small amount, even just to try it — the starting assumption should be that you need to look into New Zealand's excise licensing requirements first, rather than assuming a hobby exemption exists the way it does for home-brewed beer. See "What licences do you need to start a distillery in New Zealand?" for the licensing side of this.
