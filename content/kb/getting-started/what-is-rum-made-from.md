Rum is a sugar cane spirit, but there are two quite different starting points that shape its whole character.

## Molasses-based rum

Most rum in the world — including most Caribbean rum — is made from **molasses**, the dark, viscous syrup left over after sugar cane juice has been boiled down and crystallised sugar removed. Molasses is cheap, shelf-stable and easy to ship, which is part of why it became the dominant base historically. It tends to produce richer, heavier styles once aged.

## Cane juice rum (rhum agricole)

Some rum, most famously from the French Caribbean (rhum agricole), is distilled directly from **fresh-pressed sugar cane juice** rather than molasses. This style tends to be more grassy, vegetal and aromatic, because it skips the boiling/sugar-extraction step that molasses goes through.

## Fermentation and distillation

Whichever base is used, it's fermented with yeast (from a few days to several weeks depending on style) and then distilled — typically in pot stills for richer, more characterful rums, or column stills for lighter, cleaner styles. Ageing in oak (or not) then determines whether it becomes a white/unaged rum, a golden rum, or a dark, long-aged rum.

New Zealand doesn't grow sugar cane, so local rum producers import molasses or cane spirit as a base and do the fermentation, distillation, blending and (in some cases) ageing here — see our Rum, Vodka and Moonshine section for more detail.
