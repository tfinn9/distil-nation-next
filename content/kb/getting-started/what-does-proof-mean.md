"Proof" predates the modern ABV system, and confusingly, it doesn't mean the same thing everywhere it's used.

## US proof

In the United States, proof is simply **double the ABV**. A spirit bottled at 40% ABV is "80 proof." A cask-strength whisky at 60% ABV would be "120 proof." This is a straightforward conversion: `proof = ABV × 2`.

## Historical UK proof

The old British system was more complicated and based on the density of the spirit relative to water at a specific temperature — "100 proof" in the old UK system was actually about 57.1% ABV, not 50%. The UK officially replaced this system with ABV labelling in 1980.

## Where the word comes from

The term dates back to a rough test where gunpowder was soaked in spirit and set alight — if it still burned, the spirit was "proved" to contain enough alcohol. Spirit that failed to ignite was considered under-proof.

## What New Zealand uses today

New Zealand labelling uses **ABV**, not proof, so you generally won't see "proof" on a locally produced bottle. You'll mostly encounter the term when reading about American whiskey (bourbon, rye) or older/imported spirits.
