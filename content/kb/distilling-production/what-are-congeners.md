"Congener" is the umbrella term for all the chemical compounds in a spirit besides ethanol and water — the things that actually give a spirit its distinctive taste, smell and colour.

## Where congeners come from

- **Fermentation** produces esters and higher (fusel) alcohols, contributing fruity, floral or heavier flavour notes (see "How does yeast affect flavour?").
- **Distillation** determines how many of those compounds carry through into the final spirit, and in what concentration — a wider "cut" (see "What are heads, hearts and tails?") generally retains more congeners than a narrow one.
- **Maturation** adds further congeners extracted from the barrel itself — tannins, vanillin, and other wood-derived compounds (see "How does barrel aging work?").

## Why congeners matter

A spirit with essentially no congeners — like a heavily rectified, well-filtered vodka — tastes clean and neutral by design. A spirit with a high congener content — like a heavy, pot-distilled dark rum or a cask-strength whisky — tastes rich, complex and full of character, precisely because of everything beyond pure ethanol that's present.

## The hangover connection

Congeners are also frequently discussed in relation to hangover severity — some research has suggested that drinks higher in congeners (like dark spirits, red wine, and darker beers) may be associated with worse hangover symptoms than very clean, congener-light drinks (like vodka or gin) consumed at an equivalent amount of alcohol, though individual factors and overall alcohol consumed matter far more than congener content alone.

## Not inherently good or bad

More congeners doesn't automatically mean "better" — it just means more character, for better or worse depending on how well the fermentation, distillation and maturation were managed. A well-made, congener-light vodka and a well-made, congener-rich cask-strength whisky are both "good" spirits — they're just built around opposite goals.
