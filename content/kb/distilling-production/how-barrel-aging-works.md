Barrel ageing isn't passive storage — it's an active chemical process, and it's responsible for most of the colour and a huge share of the final flavour in spirits like whisky, aged rum and brandy.

## 1. Extraction

Wood contains compounds — tannins, lignin (which breaks down into vanillin, giving vanilla notes), and sugars caramelised during barrel charring or toasting — that dissolve into the spirit over time. This is where most of a barrel-aged spirit's colour, and flavours like vanilla, caramel, toffee and spice, actually come from.

## 2. Oxidation

Barrels aren't airtight — the wood is slightly porous, allowing a slow exchange of air. Over time, this oxygen exposure changes some of the spirit's chemistry, generally softening harsher notes and contributing to a rounder, more mellow character the longer maturation continues.

## 3. Evaporation ("the angel's share")

Some of the spirit is lost to evaporation through the porous wood over time — see our dedicated article on the angel's share. This concentrates the remaining spirit and its flavour compounds, which is part of why very long-aged spirits often taste richer and more intense.

## Why the type of barrel matters so much

What the barrel is made from, how it was toasted or charred, and whether it previously held another spirit (like bourbon, sherry or wine) all significantly change what gets extracted — see "What types of wood are used in barrel aging?" and "Why do different barrels create different flavours?" for more detail.

## Climate accelerates or slows the whole process

Warmer, more variable climates generally speed up all three of these mechanisms — extraction, oxidation and evaporation — compared to cooler, more stable ones, which is a major reason why the same spirit can mature very differently depending on where it's stored (see "How long does whisky need to age?").
