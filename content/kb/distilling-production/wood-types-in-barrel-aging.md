Oak is, by a wide margin, the standard wood used for maturing spirits, and for good practical reasons: it's strong enough to hold liquid under pressure over long periods, tight-grained enough to prevent leaking while still allowing the slow oxygen exchange maturation needs, and rich in flavour-contributing compounds.

## American white oak

The most common oak used globally for spirit maturation, including virtually all bourbon barrels (which, by regulation, must be new and charred). American white oak tends to contribute strong vanilla, caramel and coconut-like notes, partly because of its naturally higher vanillin content compared to European oak.

## European oak

Common in sherry and wine cask production, and widely used for maturing (or finishing) Scotch whisky. European oak tends to bring more tannin, spice and dried-fruit character, and is often tighter-grained, which changes the extraction profile compared to American oak.

## Charring vs toasting

Barrels are typically either **charred** (exposed to direct flame, creating a layer of charcoal on the inside surface) or **toasted** (exposed to gentler heat without charring). Charring is standard for bourbon and contributes a caramelised, smoky-sweet layer; toasting is more common for wine and sherry casks and tends to give a gentler, more delicate wood influence.

## Other woods

Non-oak woods (like chestnut in some traditional European spirit production) appear occasionally but are a small minority — oak's balance of strength, flavour and long tradition has made it the near-universal default across whisky, rum, brandy and other barrel-aged spirits worldwide.
