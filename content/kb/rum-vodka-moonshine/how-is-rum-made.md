Rum's process is simpler than whisky's in some ways (no malting step) but shares the same fermentation-distillation-maturation backbone.

## 1. Choose the sugar source

Rum starts from either **molasses** (the thick by-product left after boiling down sugar cane juice and extracting crystallised sugar) or **fresh-pressed cane juice** (used for rhum agricole-style rums). See "What is molasses?" and "What is the difference between white and dark rum?" for how this choice shapes the final product.

## 2. Fermentation

Yeast is added to the diluted molasses or cane juice and left to ferment, typically for a few days up to a couple of weeks depending on the style being targeted. Slower, longer fermentations tend to develop more complex, funky flavour compounds; faster fermentations produce a cleaner, lighter spirit.

## 3. Distillation

The fermented liquid ("wash") is distilled either in **pot stills** (which retain more flavour and character, typical of richer, heavier rum styles) or **column stills** (which produce a lighter, cleaner spirit, typical of light rums destined for mixing).

## 4. Ageing (or not)

- **White/unaged rum** is bottled shortly after distillation, sometimes lightly filtered to remove any colour picked up during a brief rest.
- **Golden and dark rum** is aged in oak barrels — sometimes former bourbon or whisky casks — for anywhere from a year or two up to several decades, developing colour and flavour from the wood in the same way whisky does (see "How does barrel aging work?").

## 5. Blending and bottling

Many rums are blends of multiple casks or ages to hit a consistent house style; others are bottled as single-cask or single-vintage releases. Some producers add a small amount of caramel colouring for visual consistency across batches, which is legal in many markets but worth being aware of when comparing colour across brands.
