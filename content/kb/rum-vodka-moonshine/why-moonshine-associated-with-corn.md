Moonshine's link to corn comes from geography and economics as much as flavour preference.

## Corn was abundant and cheap in the Southern US

The Appalachian region and other parts of the southern and central United States — the heartland of the historical moonshine tradition — grew corn abundantly. For farmers, distilling surplus corn into spirit was a practical way to preserve a perishable crop and turn it into something more valuable and transportable than raw grain.

## It was also a Prohibition-era necessity

During and after Prohibition (1920–1933) in the United States, illicit distilling boomed, and corn — already the dominant local crop — remained the default base grain for both economic and practical reasons: it was locally available, didn't require importing other grains, and could be fermented and distilled with fairly simple equipment.

## Corn also gives a distinctive character

Beyond practicality, corn-based spirit has a naturally sweet, slightly oily character that came to define the "moonshine" flavour profile most people associate with the term — the same base that, if aged in charred new oak, becomes bourbon (see "What is the difference between whisky, whiskey, bourbon and rye?").

## Not the only grain used

While corn is the classic association, moonshine (in the broader sense of "illicitly or informally distilled spirit") has historically been made from whatever grain or sugar source was locally available — including rye, wheat, sugar, or fruit, depending on the region and era.
