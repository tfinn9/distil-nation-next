Creating a new gin recipe is closer to perfumery than most people expect — it's a slow, iterative process of testing individual ingredients and then testing how they interact.

## 1. Start with a concept

Most recipes start with an idea rather than a random list of botanicals — a flavour direction (citrus-forward, floral, spicy), a story (showcasing a specific native ingredient or local landscape), or a gap the distiller wants to fill in their range.

## 2. Test botanicals individually

Distillers often make small single-botanical test distillations or tinctures first, to understand exactly what character each ingredient brings on its own — its intensity, how it behaves in alcohol, and how it changes with heat during distillation versus simple cold infusion.

## 3. Build and test combinations

Botanicals are then combined in small test batches, adjusting ratios and testing how ingredients interact — some botanicals amplify or clash with others, and a botanical that tastes great alone can disappear or turn unpleasant in combination with certain others.

## 4. Test the distillation method

The same botanical bill can taste different depending on whether it's steeped before distillation, vapour-infused, added post-distillation, or some combination — so distillers often test the same recipe through multiple methods before settling on one.

## 5. Scale up carefully

Once a recipe is locked at small-batch scale, distillers need to test that it scales predictably to full production size, since the ratio of surface area, heat distribution and contact time inside a still can behave differently at larger volumes.

## 6. Taste, tweak, repeat

Most new gin recipes go through many iterations — sometimes dozens — before a distiller is satisfied enough to release it commercially.
