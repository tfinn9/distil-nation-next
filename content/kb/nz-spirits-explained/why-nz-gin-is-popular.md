Gin's rise in New Zealand tracks a global "gin renaissance" that's been building for well over a decade, but a few local factors have amplified it here specifically.

## It's the easiest spirit to start with

Unlike whisky, gin doesn't need years of barrel ageing before it can be sold — a distillery can go from first distillation to first sale in a matter of weeks. That makes it the natural first product for new craft distilleries, which is a big part of why gin dominates the New Zealand spirits landscape today (see "What spirits are New Zealand distilleries best known for?").

## Native botanicals give it a genuine point of difference

Because gin's flavour comes largely from whatever botanicals are added to the base spirit, New Zealand distillers can build genuinely distinctive products using local, often native ingredients — mānuka, kawakawa, horopito, and native citrus among them. That's a much harder differentiation to achieve with whisky or vodka, where the production process itself does more of the flavour work.

## It suits New Zealand's food and hospitality culture

Gin pairs naturally with New Zealand's strong café, bar and restaurant culture, and lends itself to easy tonic-and-garnish serving that doesn't require deep spirits knowledge to enjoy — making it an accessible entry point for casual drinkers as well as enthusiasts.

## It rode a global wave

The broader international "gin boom" of the 2010s created strong consumer appetite and retail shelf space for new, small-batch gin brands well before New Zealand's local scene matured — local producers benefited from a category that was already growing in popularity worldwide.
